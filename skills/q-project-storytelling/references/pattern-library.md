# Pattern Library

Use these patterns as idea lenses, not as copied templates. Keep the final
wording tailored to the user's project, audience, and evidence.

## Product / Adoption Narrative

Use when the goal is to persuade colleagues or users to try something.

- Target audience: who has the pain?
- Current alternative: what do they do today?
- Pain: what breaks, slows down, or creates risk?
- Promise: what gets better?
- Differentiator: why this approach is credible or unusual?
- Proof: what evidence exists?
- First action: how do they start with low risk?

Good for: internal tools, workflows, skills, onboarding, release candidates,
demo proposals, and adoption pilots.

## Architecture Narrative

Use when explaining how something works.

- Context: users, surrounding systems, constraints.
- Capability view: major responsibilities and boundaries.
- Component view: skills, scripts, services, files, data stores, or teams.
- Flow view: how work moves through the system.
- Risk view: what can fail and how the design contains it.
- Evolution view: what can be replaced, extended, or retired.

Good for: software architecture, agent workflows, build systems, MCU demos,
toolchains, and validation infrastructure.

## Project Story Narrative

Use when reporting a project or milestone.

- Before: what was hard or unreliable?
- Trigger: what made the work necessary now?
- Intervention: what changed?
- Evidence: how do we know it worked?
- Decision: what is stable, experimental, or rejected?
- Next: what is the next practical step?

Good for: status decks, milestone reviews, lessons learned, internal project
introductions, and handoff notes.

## Science / Complex-Concept Narrative

Use when the audience may not share the technical background.

- Start with the real-world question or decision.
- Define only the terms needed for the next step.
- Use one concrete analogy or example, but do not let the analogy become the
  proof.
- Move from observable behavior to mechanism to implication.
- Separate what is known, what is inferred, and what remains uncertain.

Good for: technical education, architecture popularization, research summaries,
customer-facing simplification, and cross-functional onboarding.

## Messaging Checks

Run these before generating the final artifact:

- Could the audience repeat the one-line promise?
- Does every section answer "why should I care?"
- Are claims supported by visible proof or explicitly labeled as candidates?
- Are details sequenced from context to mechanism to evidence?
- Is the adoption path concrete enough to try?
- Is the wording stable across slides, notes, and handoff docs?
